Selected work

Three systems I built and ran. Each one: what was wrong, what I decided, what happened.

Basket (wntd.ai) · 2024–2026 · Lead engineer, technical owner

Rebuilding the platform behind a shopping app

Basket users save products from any online shop, and the app has to know the current price. When I took over, that job ran on 80+ serverless functions across 3 clouds and 5 data stores. One failed page left traces in 8 to 10 log streams.

I designed a replacement: one core library with no cloud code, one PostgreSQL row per URL and one trace per request. AI agents wrote the code to my design and under my review, in about two weeks. Both platforms ran on the same batches before I switched production.

  • 85% → 95%+success rate
  • 25 s → 3 sper page
  • 80%+lower cost

Locket · 2020–2024 · Founding engineer, then CTO

From the first line of code to CTO of an insurer

Locket is a UK home insurer regulated by the FCA. I built the B2C product from the start: a customer could buy a policy in about 2 minutes, where other insurers needed 15 or more. The pricing engine, in Elixir, called data providers in parallel and answered in 1 to 2 seconds.

As CTO I designed the B2B platform that other insurers ran on: a PostgreSQL database for each tenant, Kong checking a JWT for each tenant, services on EKS. A customer could install it into its own AWS account in one step. I led a team of about 15.

How I work

Each habit comes from a specific system and a specific number.

  1. Measure before you spend.

    At Basket we assumed most failed pages were bot blocks. When we measured every attempt by the data it extracted, blocks caused under 5% of failures. The engineering time went to extraction instead.

  2. Success means you have the data.

    A 200 status proves nothing. Some shops return 200 for a bot challenge and 404 for a page that still has the product. A fetch counts only when the extractor gets the title, the price and the currency.

  3. Cheapest method first.

    Paid proxies are the last step on the ladder, never the default. Traffic to them fell by about 95%, and one vendor bill went from $3,000 to $975 a month.

  4. A backup is done when the restore works.

    We restored the vector database backups on a separate cluster to prove it. At Locket I started an on-call rotation with runbooks and incident drills.

  5. Delete before you add.

    Unused resources cost money every month. At Basket I removed about 1,200 unused AWS resources and 100,000 lines of old code.

  6. Agents write the code. I own the design and the review.

    The setup took a year: repository instructions, a shared skill library, read-only data access and a model for each task. After that, the designer and the CEO shipped features under my review.
